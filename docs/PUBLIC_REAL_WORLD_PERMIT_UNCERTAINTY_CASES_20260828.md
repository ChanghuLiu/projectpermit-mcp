# Public real-world permit-uncertainty cases — 2026-08-28

## Purpose

Look for real homeowner/contractor workflow examples where permit applicability or jurisdiction-specific requirements were missed, discovered late, or materially affected quote/construction execution.

These are public anecdotal cases, found by searching for permit-related experiences. They are **not representative E2/E3 evidence** and should not be used to estimate incidence.

## Case 1 — permit requirement distinguished one bidder from the others

Source: HomeStars review on the P & P General Contractors profile.

The homeowner reports comparing contractors for a renovation involving taking walls down. According to the review, P & P was the **only contractor** who mentioned that an engineer would be required for the structural work and that a permit was needed. During execution, the city also requested an **unplanned plumbing inspection**, causing a one-day drywall delay.

Public source:
https://www.homestars.com/profile/2776147-p-p-renovations/reviews?page=10

### What it supports

- permit/engineering applicability can be a differentiating fact **during quote evaluation**, not only during filing;
- multiple contractors quoting the same scope may not surface the same compliance requirements;
- unexpected permit/inspection dependencies can alter scheduling after work begins.

### Limits

- project municipality is not explicitly stated in the retrieved review text; do not infer it solely from the provider's profile location;
- no denominator is available for how often this occurs;
- it does not prove that an API would have changed contractor behavior.

## Case 2 — contractor transferred Toronto assumptions into another municipality

Source: HomeStars review for New Canadian Drain & Plumbing.

The homeowner describes foundation-related work in the Town of Halton Hills. The contractor reportedly did not initially want to obtain a building permit because this type of work was commonly handled without one in Toronto. The homeowner contacted Halton Hills and was told a permit was needed, then arranged and paid for the permit independently.

Public source:
https://www.homestars.com/profile/217845-new-canadian-drain-plumbing-ltd/reviews?page=123

### What it supports

- **municipality-specific transfer error is real**: experience in one city can create a wrong assumption in another;
- a cross-jurisdiction decision layer has a plausible operational role.

### Limits

- Halton Hills is outside ProjectPermit's current seven-city footprint;
- the scope is not necessarily one of the current priority families;
- this is a single older anecdote, not frequency evidence.

## Case 3 — permit discovery caused scope reduction after work started

Source: HomeStars review for D.E.H Inc.

A homeowner/customer reports a small office renovation where the planned scope had to be reduced after discovering that a city permit was required to install a room-dividing partition. The review explicitly criticizes the contractor for not knowing this before the project began.

Public source:
https://www.homestars.com/profile/2911278-d-e-h-inc/reviews?page=3

### What it supports

- late applicability discovery can change the actual project scope;
- the economic effect can occur before filing complexity becomes the main problem.

### Limits

- this is commercial work, outside ProjectPermit's present residential focus;
- the municipality is not established in the retrieved text;
- it is an anecdote selected because it mentions a permit problem.

## Case 4 — work completed without permit created later transaction risk

Source: HomeStars review for K H Davis Engineering Consultants.

The reviewer says a recently purchased home's sale was contingent on determining whether foundation work completed **without a permit** had been done correctly. An engineering firm was then engaged and obtained a permit retroactively.

Public source:
https://www.homestars.com/profile/2781222-KH-Davis-Engineering-Consultants-Ltd/reviews?page=3

### What it supports

- missed permit obligations can create downstream property-transaction and remediation costs;
- compliance uncertainty can have value beyond the construction moment.

### Limits

- downstream remediation is not ProjectPermit's intended primary workflow;
- not frequency evidence.

## Evidence interpretation

The strongest qualitative signal is not simply that permits are difficult. It is that:

> the permit question can be unresolved or inconsistently recognized **at quote/scope time**, and municipality-specific assumptions can fail when transferred across jurisdictions.

That matches ProjectPermit's intended boundary better than generic permit-filing pain.

However, because these cases were found through permit-problem searches, they suffer strong selection bias. They do **not** answer:

- what percentage of ordinary renovation leads have unresolved applicability;
- whether that share is high enough to sustain >=500 candidate calls/month in one workflow;
- whether deterministic preflight is materially better than an advisor/contractor asking the municipality;
- whether a platform will pay for the capability.
